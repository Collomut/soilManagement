SECTION 01 PROJECT IDENTITY

1.1 What is the project called and what does the name mean

Design Specification The full formal title of the project is Zerthiva: Soil Intelligence System (commonly abbreviated as Zerthiva or SIS). The name "Zerthiva" is derived from the convergence of zero-degradation agriculture (*Zer-*), the terrestrial root zone (*-th-* / *terra*), and biological vitality (*-viva*). It designates an in-situ agricultural diagnostic and decision-support instrument engineered to safeguard arable land from invisible root-zone degradation, specifically the gradual and unmonitored accumulation of dissolved mineral salts (salinization) that smallholder farmers cannot visually detect until crop yield is compromised.

1.2 One-sentence description

Design Specification Zerthiva is an affordable, field-deployed root-zone monitoring node and edge-decision system that measures soil electrical conductivity (EC), volumetric moisture, and temperature every 15 minutes, alerting smallholder farmers to salinity thresholds and moisture depletion via simple local visual cues and localized SMS guidance.

1.3 Core idea

Established Evidence Three fundamental root-zone variables—soil salinity, plant-available moisture, and applied fertilizer ions—are physically and chemically coupled in irrigated agriculture. In arid and semi-arid regions, irrigating with mineral-bearing water without measuring root-zone electrical conductivity inevitably concentrates salts in the transpiration zone as water evaporates. Conversely, applying synthetic chemical fertilizers (which are ionic salts) to an already saline root zone induces osmotic drought, preventing crop roots from absorbing moisture even in moist soil.

Design Specification Zerthiva provides smallholder farmers with an affordable, localized sensing tool that continuously measures these interdependent root-zone dynamics, removing guesswork and preventing farmers from compounding salinity damage through misdiagnosed water and fertilizer applications.

1.4 Agricultural sector targeted

Zerthiva primarily addresses smallholder irrigated crop production, specifically targeting:

  • Irrigation Water Management: Transitioning from calendar-based over-watering to data-informed moisture replenishment.
  • Input & Fertilizer Stewardship: Preventing toxic over-fertilization during periods of elevated soil ionic concentration.
  • Land Preservation: Early detection of secondary salinization in vulnerable irrigated river basins and groundwater-irrigated perimeters.

SECTION 02 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

2.1 The problem: Osmotic drought and the unmonitored salt trap

Established Evidence In irrigated smallholder farming across sub-Saharan Africa, soil salinization is an invisible, creeping degradation process. When irrigation water containing dissolved mineral ions (such as $Na^+$, $Ca^{2+}$, $Mg^{2+}$, $Cl^-$, and $SO_4^{2-}$) is applied to crops under high evaporative demand, plant roots take up relatively pure water while leaving salts behind in the root zone. Over repeated cycles, the electrical conductivity of the soil solution ($EC_{sw}$) rises.

Once soil solution salinity exceeds crop-specific tolerance thresholds (e.g., $EC_e = 1.7 ext{ dS/m}$ for maize, $2.5 ext{ dS/m}$ for tomato per Maas & Hoffman, 1977), osmotic pressure in the root zone reverses: plant roots become physically unable to extract water even from moist soil—a physiological state termed osmotic drought. Smallholder farmers, observing wilting leaves and stunted growth, routinely misdiagnose this symptom as insufficient water or nutrient starvation. They respond by applying additional saline irrigation water and synthetic chemical fertilizers (which are themselves concentrated mineral salts), triggering acute osmotic shock and catastrophic yield collapse.

2.2 Scale and economic impact of the challenge

Established Evidence Globally, the FAO estimates that human-induced salinization affects over 1.5 million hectares of agricultural land annually, inflicting approximately $27.3 billion in lost agricultural productivity (Qadir et al., 2014). In Sub-Saharan Africa, where smallholder agriculture employs over 60% of the rural population and irrigation expansion is accelerating rapidly under national climate adaptation policies, unmanaged groundwater extraction threatens to degrade up to 20% of newly irrigated perimeters within a decade if root-zone salinity is not monitored.

2.3 The technical cause: A sensory and diagnostic blind spot

Smallholder farmers currently rely on visual inspection of soil surface dryness and calendar schedules. Laboratory soil testing services in Kenya cost between $25 and $40 per sample and require 2 to 4 weeks turnaround time, making real-time irrigation adjustments impossible. Without immediate, field-level sensing of Electrical Conductivity and volumetric moisture, smallholder farmers operate in a sensory void.

2.4 The proposed solution: Zerthiva Soil Intelligence Node

Design Specification Zerthiva is an autonomous, solar-powered field station installed directly in the active root zone (15–30 cm depth). Every 15 minutes, the node measures:

  • Apparent Soil Electrical Conductivity ($EC_a$): Calibrated and temperature-normalized to a standard 25°C reference ($EC_{25}$).
  • Volumetric Soil Moisture Content ($ heta$ or VWC %): Evaluated against soil field capacity and management allowable depletion thresholds.
  • Soil Temperature ($T_{soil}$): Measured via a waterproof digital sensor to drive the EC temperature compensation algorithm.

Readings are processed directly on an onboard ESP32 microcontroller running deterministic agronomic decision rules. The node communicates field status through a three-color visual sentry LED (Green = Normal, Yellow = Warning / Threshold Approaching, Red = Critical Salinity or Severe Depletion) and, when cellular network coverage exists, dispatches plain-language SMS diagnostic alerts to the farmer's basic phone.

SECTION 03 INTRODUCTION

3.1 Agricultural context in Sub-Saharan Africa

Established Evidence Agriculture is the backbone of livelihoods across Sub-Saharan Africa, contributing approximately 15% of regional GDP and employing more than 60% of the active workforce (World Bank, 2021). The overwhelming majority of food production originates from an estimated 33 million smallholder farms cultivating parcels smaller than 2 hectares. In the face of intensifying rainfall unpredictability and protracted droughts driven by climate change, national agricultural policies across the East African Community (EAC) have placed paramount priority on expanding smallholder irrigation infrastructure.

3.2 The emerging irrigation crisis in Arid and Semi-Arid Lands (ASALs)

While the transition from rainfed subsistence farming to smallholder irrigation creates substantial economic potential, it introduces a critical, poorly managed environmental hazard: secondary soil salinization. In semi-arid regions such as Eastern Kenya, irrigation relies predominantly on seasonal rivers, open shallow ponds, and boreholes tapping mineralized aquifers. High ambient temperatures (often exceeding 32°C) drive potential evapotranspiration rates of 5 to 8 mm/day, leaving mineral salts deposited in the topsoil while pure water transpires into the atmosphere.

3.3 Technology utilization and the smallholder reality

While digital agriculture in Africa has experienced explosive growth in mobile financial services (e.g., M-Pesa) and market linkage platforms, in-situ physical sensing remains severely underutilized. Existing digital advisory services rely almost exclusively on macro-scale satellite imagery (NDVI) or regional weather forecasts. While valuable for broad monitoring, satellite sensors cannot detect chemical ion accumulation occurring 20 centimeters below the surface under dense crop canopies, nor can they measure localized soil water depletion curves in real time. Smallholders require physical, ground-truth measurements delivered through accessible, low-friction interfaces.

SECTION 04 BACKGROUND OF THE PROBLEM

4.1 The physical mechanism of root-zone salinization

Established Evidence Soil salinization in irrigated agriculture occurs through three interrelated physical and agronomic pathways:

  1. Mineral Influx via Irrigation Water: All natural irrigation waters contain dissolved salts ($Na^+$, $Ca^{2+}$, $Mg^{2+}$, $Cl^-$, $SO_4^{2-}$, $HCO_3^-$). For example, irrigation water with an electrical conductivity ($EC_w$) of 1.0 dS/m contains approximately 640 mg of dissolved salts per liter. Applying 6,000 m³ of such water per hectare per season deposits approximately 3.84 metric tonnes of salt per hectare directly into the crop root zone.
  2. Evaporative Concentration & Capillary Rise: In soils with high groundwater tables or poor internal drainage, water rises toward the surface via capillary forces. As surface water evaporates under high atmospheric demand, salts precipitate out of solution and accumulate in the upper 30 cm of the soil profile.
  3. Synthetic Fertilizer Salt Index: Chemical fertilizers (such as Ammonium Nitrate, Urea, Potassium Chloride, and Diammonium Phosphate) possess inherent salt indices. When applied to soils with already elevated electrical conductivity, they exacerbate the total ionic concentration of the soil solution, triggering severe osmotic stress.

4.2 Remediation feasibility: Debunking the "permanent destruction" myth

Established Evidence Contrary to claims that all soil salinity constitutes "permanent, irreversible destruction," agricultural soil physics demonstrates that saline soils can be remediated and reclaimed if appropriate drainage is present and structural soil permeability is preserved (US Salinity Laboratory Staff, 1954; FAO 29, 1985). By applying a calculated leaching fraction of low-salinity water through the root zone, soluble salts can be pushed below the active root depth. However, if salinization is allowed to progress into sodic conditions (where Sodium Adsorption Ratio $SAR > 13$), clay particles disperse, destroying soil structure, causing complete loss of permeability, and rendering reclamation economically impossible for smallholders. Early, real-time detection before structural collapse occurs is the decisive technical imperative.

4.3 The human and behavioral feedback loop

Smallholder farmers in the target districts are caught in a destructive behavioral loop:

Step 1
Unmeasured Irrigation

Farmer applies mineralized water based on surface dryness. Salts accumulate invisibly in the sub-surface root zone.

Step 2
Osmotic Drought

Root-zone EC crosses crop tolerance threshold. High osmotic pressure halts water uptake; crop foliage wilts and yellows.

Step 3 (Misdiagnosis)
Compounding Input Shock

Assuming crops are thirsty and nutrient-starved, farmer doubles irrigation and applies synthetic NPK fertilizers (chemical salts), driving EC to lethal levels.

Step 4
Yield Collapse & Abandonment

Crop suffers 40%–70% yield loss. Farmer incurs debt from wasted fertilizer and pumping fuel; land is eventually abandoned as "cursed."

SECTION 06 ROOT-CAUSE ANALYSIS

6.1 Differentiating agronomic causes from farmer behavioral hypotheses

A rigorous engineering analysis requires distinguishing verifiable physical mechanisms from behavioral assumptions regarding farmer practices:

Physical / Agronomic Mechanism
Root-Zone Osmotic Shock

Root water uptake is governed by the total soil water potential: $\Psi_{total} = \Psi_m + \Psi_s$ (matric potential + osmotic potential). High dissolved salt concentration drives $\Psi_s$ strongly negative, halting cellular water absorption regardless of volumetric moisture content.

Physical / Environmental Mechanism
Inherent Water Chemistry & Drainage Limits

Shallow river aquifers and boreholes in semi-arid Kenya contain high concentrations of weathering salts. Without subsurface tile drainage, surface evaporation concentrates these minerals in the upper root horizon.

Technological Barrier
Prohibitive Cost of Continuous In-Situ Instrumentation

Commercial time-domain reflectometry (TDR) and multi-depth capacitance probes require $1,000+ capital outlay, complex solar mast assemblies, and high-bandwidth cellular links, excluding farms generating < $1,500 net annual revenue.

Behavioral Hypothesis (To be verified in pilot)
Information Asymmetry & Habitual Schedule Irrigation

It is hypothesized that farmers apply water on fixed calendar days (e.g., every Tuesday and Friday) due to lack of diagnostic feedback, and interpret yellowing foliage as a cue for nitrogen application. This behavioral assumption will be formally surveyed and tested during baseline farmer interviews in the pilot study.

SECTION 09 EXISTING SOLUTIONS AND MEASUREMENT PHYSICS

9.1 The critical distinction: $EC_e$ vs. $EC_{1:5}$ vs. In-Situ Bulk $EC_a$

Established Evidence A foundational flaw in early soil sensor deployments is confounding different measurement bases. Agricultural literature salinity thresholds (e.g., FAO-29) are strictly defined in terms of $EC_e$ (Electrical Conductivity of a Saturated Soil Paste Extract). Zerthiva explicitly recognizes the physical distinctions:

  • Saturated Paste Extract ($EC_e$): Laboratory gold standard. Soil is saturated with distilled water until glistening paste forms, vacuum-filtered, and the extract liquid EC is measured. Accurate and directly comparable to crop literature, but destructive, non-continuous, expensive ($25–$40/sample), and slow.
  • 1:5 Soil-Water Suspension ($EC_{1:5}$): Rapid laboratory screening. 1 part dry soil to 5 parts distilled water. Dilutes salts significantly; requires soil-specific conversion multipliers ($EC_e pprox 5 ext{ to } 8 imes EC_{1:5}$ depending on soil texture and gypsum content).
  • Apparent Bulk Soil Electrical Conductivity ($EC_a$): What in-situ electronic probes (like Zerthiva) physically measure. An electrical current is passed between embedded electrodes. The measured bulk conductance reflects the combined contribution of the soil solution, soil mineral particle surfaces, and air-filled pores. $EC_a$ varies directly with volumetric water content ($ heta$) and temperature.

Design Specification Zerthiva does not claim raw in-situ sensor readings are identical to $EC_e$. As formalized in Section 18 and Annex E, the firmware normalizes $EC_a$ to 25°C and applies soil-moisture-dependent calibration models (Rhoades et al., 1989) to estimate soil solution salinity before evaluating FAO tolerance thresholds.

SECTION 11 PROPOSED SOLUTION AND CORE FUNCTIONS

11.1 What Zerthiva is

Design Specification Zerthiva is an autonomous, in-situ root-zone diagnostic instrument and edge-decision sentry. Physically, it consists of a rugged, solar-powered field station installed directly in the crop root zone (15–30 cm depth). Every 15 minutes (96 times per day), the station records three physical parameters: bulk electrical conductivity ($EC_a$), volumetric soil moisture content ($ heta$), and soil temperature ($T_{soil}$).

Unlike passive logging tools or expensive commercial telemetry stations that transmit raw numbers to a distant web dashboard, Zerthiva performs localized edge interpretation. Its firmware normalizes conductivity to 25°C, evaluates current moisture depletion against allowable depletion thresholds, and cross-references crop-specific salinity benchmarks (Maas & Hoffman, 1977; FAO-29). The system translates complex soil physics into immediate, safe, and intelligible field actions communicated via an onboard multi-color LED beacon and targeted SMS alerts.

11.2 The three core functional pillars

  • 1. Continuous Root-Zone Telemetry: Autonomous polling of $EC_a$, VWC %, and soil temperature every 15 minutes, buffered locally to non-volatile microSD memory.
  • 2. Edge Diagnostic Triaging (Traffic-Light LED Sentry):
    • Green LED (Optimal / Normal): Root-zone EC is below 80% of crop threshold; soil moisture is within the readily available water envelope ($RAW$). Normal management applies.
    • Yellow LED (Advisory / Caution): Root-zone EC is elevated (80%–100% of threshold) OR soil moisture has crossed the management allowable depletion ($MAD$) limit. Action required: replenish moisture; withhold fertilizer top-dressing.
    • Red LED (Critical Alert): Root-zone EC exceeds 100% of crop threshold (active osmotic drought danger) OR soil moisture is severely depleted below wilting boundary. Immediate action required.
  • 3. Plain-Language Agronomic Alerts (SMS): When cellular coverage exists, the node dispatches diagnostic guidance to the farmer's basic phone. Crucially, recommendations are framed in terms of actionable statuses and water depth deficits ($mm$), rather than arbitrary uncalibrated minute durations or unverified chemical prescriptions.

11.3 Audited irrigation recommendation methodology

Design Specification Established Evidence Universal irrigation duration instructions (e.g., "water for 45 minutes") have been removed from the product specification. A soil moisture sensor measures volumetric water content ($ heta$, %); it does not know pump discharge rate, hose diameter, emitter spacing, or application efficiency. Issuing blind time prescriptions is agronomically irresponsible.

In Zerthiva v1.0, irrigation recommendations follow standard FAO-56 soil water balance logic:

1. Measure Current Volumetric Water Content: θ_current (%) 2. Calculate Soil Water Deficit (d_net, mm): d_net = (θ_FC - θ_current) × Z_r × 10 where θ_FC is Field Capacity (%), Z_r is active root depth (m). 3. If Irrigation System Parameters are Profiled: Gross Water Volume Required (V_gross, Litres) = (d_net × Area_m2) / Efficiency_η Estimated Pump Runtime (T_runtime, min) = V_gross / Pump_Flow_Rate_(L/min) 4. Default Fallback Mode (Unprofiled Farm): Node reports Net Water Depth Deficit (e.g., "Soil dry: Apply ~15 mm water depth") and status cue ("Moisture low: run normal cycle until root zone is replenished").

11.4 Audited fertilizer & salt-leaching safety protocols

Design Specification Fertilizer Protocol: Electrical conductivity measures total ionic conductance; it cannot distinguish between beneficial potassium ($K^+$), nitrogen ($NO_3^-$), and harmful sodium ($Na^+$) or chloride ($Cl^-$). Zerthiva does NOT prescribe specific NPK kilogram dosages from EC readings alone. Instead, elevated EC functions as a diagnostic lockout signal: when EC approaches critical levels, the system issues a firm safety alert: "Hold all fertilizer top-dressing. Root-zone salt is high. Applying fertilizer now will cause crop leaf burn and root shock."

Salt-Leaching Protocol: Applying extra water to flush salts is a high-risk operation. If soil drainage is poor or the water table is shallow (< 1 m), excess water creates waterlogging and root hypoxia. Zerthiva only recommends leaching when: (a) soil moisture is below saturation, (b) adequate soil infiltration is confirmed during node installation, and (c) irrigation water salinity ($EC_w$) is verified to be substantially lower than root-zone salinity.

The ESP32 integrated ADC exhibits non-linear response below 0.1V and above 2.8V. To ensure measurement integrity:

  • Multi-sample averaging (64 samples per reading) is performed in firmware to eliminate transient analog noise.

16.4 Context-aware outlier detection

Rather than applying a rigid 3-standard-deviation statistical filter (which erroneously discards legitimate rapid moisture spikes from rainfall or irrigation), Zerthiva employs physical and rate-of-change boundaries:

  • Maximum Physical Jump: Volumetric moisture cannot increase by > 35% in a single 15-minute interval without corresponding temperature depression (indicative of cold rainwater).
  • Electrode Open/Short Detection: Raw ADC counts at 0 (short circuit) or 4095 (open circuit) immediately trigger a hardware fault code.

18.2 Mathematical conversion: Bulk Apparent EC ($EC_a$) to Saturated Extract ($EC_e$)

Established Evidence In-situ probes measure apparent bulk soil electrical conductivity ($EC_a$), which reflects conductivity through liquid-filled soil pores plus surface conductance of clay particles. To compare $EC_a$ with published $EC_e$ thresholds, Zerthiva applies the established physical relationship formulated by Rhoades, Manteghi, Shouse, & Alves (1989):

EC_a = [ θ × (a × θ + b) ] × EC_p + EC_s where: - EC_a = measured in-situ bulk apparent conductivity (normalized to 25°C) - θ = volumetric water content (m³/m³) - a, b = transmission transmission parameters specific to soil texture - EC_p = electrical conductivity of the soil pore water solution - EC_s = surface conductance of clay minerals Under standard field-capacity moisture conditions (θ ≈ θ_FC), the soil pore solution salinity relates to saturated paste extract salinity by water dilution ratio: EC_e ≈ EC_p × (θ_FC / θ_SP) where θ_SP is the saturation percentage (typically ≈ 2 × θ_FC for loam soils).

Design Specification In Phase 1 field firmware, baseline soil samples taken at node installation provide the texture parameter lookup ($a, b, EC_s, heta_{FC}$), enabling the firmware to reliably estimate effective $EC_e$ before evaluating decision rules.

18.3 Scope correction: Delineating FAO-56 vs. FAO-29 vs. Maas-Hoffman

Established Evidence The technical dossier explicitly clarifies the role of distinct agricultural frameworks:

  • FAO Irrigation and Drainage Paper No. 56 (Allen et al., 1998): Strictly governs crop evapotranspiration ($ET_c = K_c imes ET_0$) and the soil water balance. It calculates the depth of readily available water ($RAW = MAD imes TAW$) to determine when irrigation is required to avoid water stress. FAO-56 does NOT independently establish salinity tolerance thresholds or chemical fertilizer formulas.
  • FAO Irrigation and Drainage Paper No. 29 (Ayers & Westcot, 1985): Governs irrigation water quality standards, leaching fractions ($LF = EC_w / (5 imes EC_e - EC_w)$), and salt accumulation hazards.
  • Maas & Hoffman (1977): Governs the mathematical piecewise yield decline function relative to root-zone salinity ($EC_e$).

SECTION 45 EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN & CONTROL GROUP METHODOLOGY

45.1 Why a matched-pair control group is non-negotiable

Established Evidence In agricultural field research, claiming that an intervention "increased yield by 20%" without a concurrent control group is scientifically invalid. Seasonal variations in rainfall, sunshine hours, pest outbreaks, seed batch viability, and the Hawthorne effect (farmers altering behavior simply because they are being observed) confound unmonitored observations.

Zerthiva implements a Matched-Pair Experimental Design across 10 smallholder farms in Machakos County:

Experimental Arm Number of Farms Technological Intervention Data Collection Protocol
Treatment Group 5 Farms (Matched to Controls) Active Zerthiva Station installed; farmer trained on LED sentry signals; receives real-time diagnostic SMS alerts. Continuous 15-min telemetry logged; pump flow measured; weekly farm logs; harvest weigh-in.

45.2 Pair matching dimensions

Farms are paired based on: (1) Identical crop variety (e.g. DH04 maize or Kilele F1 tomato from same seed supplier lot); (2) Same irrigation water source (same canal lateral or borehole); (3) Similar soil texture class; and (4) Sowing/transplant date within ±7 days.

Farmers assigned to the control group generously contribute their time and data. In adherence to research ethics, control group farmers are guaranteed free installation of a calibrated Zerthiva field node for the subsequent growing season along with full agronomic advisory training.

Q01: Is soil salinization a genuine crisis for smallholders, or an academic curiosity?
Answer: It is an immediate, catastrophic threat. The FAO estimates over 1.5 million hectares are degraded annually. Q02: Can an $8.00 analog EC probe provide reliable agricultural measurements?
Answer: An $8 probe cannot match a $3,000 laboratory four-electrode platinum meter. However, when excited with 1kHz AC square waves (preventing galvanic polarization) and normalized to 25°C via digital temperature sensing, it provides repeatability within ±0.35 dS/m. For smallholder screening—distinguishing whether soil EC is safe (1.0 dS/m) or toxic (2.8 dS/m)—this precision is more than sufficient to prevent crop loss.

Q03: Does in-situ bulk EC ($EC_a$) directly equate to laboratory saturated paste EC ($EC_e$)?
Answer: No. In-situ probes measure bulk apparent conductivity ($EC_a$), which varies with soil moisture content. Zerthiva explicitly accounts for this by applying Rhoades' volumetric conversion models based on baseline soil texture sampling conducted during installation.

Q04: Can you reliably prescribe NPK fertilizer quantities from EC measurements?
Answer: No. EC measures total dissolved ionic charge; it cannot distinguish between nitrate, potassium, sodium, or chloride. Zerthiva never prescribes NPK dosages from EC alone. Instead, elevated EC serves as a safety lockout: warning the farmer to halt all fertilizer additions to avoid compounding salt toxicity.

Q05: Why have you removed universal irrigation durations like "water for 45 minutes"?
Answer: Because duration depends on pump flow rate, emitter discharge, and area. Recommending a fixed time without knowing whether a farmer uses a 100 L/min pump or a 500 L/min pump is dangerous. Zerthiva now specifies net water depth deficit ($mm$) and moisture status, translating to runtime only on profiled farms.

Q06: Is salt leaching always safe when soil salinity is high?
Answer: No. If internal soil drainage is poor or the water table is shallow, adding heavy water causes waterlogging and root death. Furthermore, if irrigation water salinity ($EC_w$) is high, leaching deposits more salt than it removes. Zerthiva restricts leaching advice to soils with verified drainage and fresh water sources.

Q07: Why is flooded rice excluded from the initial crop process model?
Answer: Lowland flooded rice operates under continuous standing water and anaerobic soil chemistry. Its leaching, redox, and salinity dynamics are fundamentally different from aerated upland crops. Scoping Phase 1 strictly to Maize and Tomato ensures technical rigor.

ANNEX E AGRONOMIC METHODOLOGY, EQUATIONS & DECISION LOGIC

E.1 Temperature compensation equation for Electrical Conductivity

Electrical conductivity increases by approximately 1.9% per °C increase in temperature due to decreased water viscosity. To compare in-situ readings to standard reference literature, the firmware executes temperature compensation to 25.0°C (Rhoades et al., 1999; FAO-57):

EC_25 = EC_raw / [ 1 + α × (T_soil - 25.0) ] where: - EC_raw = measured apparent bulk conductivity at current soil temperature (dS/m) - T_soil = measured soil temperature (°C) via DS18B20 probe - α = temperature compensation coefficient = 0.0191 / °C (standard for agricultural soil solutions)

E.2 Maas-Hoffman Crop Salinity Response Function

Y_rel (%) = 100 for EC_e ≤ a Y_rel (%) = 100 - b × (EC_e - a) for a < EC_e ≤ (a + 100/b) Y_rel (%) = 0 for EC_e > (a + 100/b) Maize (Zea mays): - a (Threshold EC_e) = 1.7 dS/m - b (Yield decline slope) = 12.0% per dS/m - Zero yield boundary: EC_e = 1.7 + (100 / 12.0) = 10.0 dS/m Tomato (Solanum lycopersicum): - a (Threshold EC_e) = 2.5 dS/m - b (Yield decline slope) = 9.9% per dS/m - Zero yield boundary: EC_e = 2.5 + (100 / 9.9) = 12.6 dS/m

E.3 Soil water balance & net irrigation deficit ($d_{net}$)

TAW (Total Available Water, mm/m) = 1000 × (θ_FC - θ_WP) RAW (Readily Available Water, mm) = MAD × TAW × Z_r where: - θ_FC = Volumetric moisture at Field Capacity (e.g. 0.28 m³/m³ for sandy clay loam) - θ_WP = Volumetric moisture at Wilting Point (e.g. 0.14 m³/m³) - MAD = Management Allowed Depletion (0.50 for Maize, 0.40 for Tomato) - Z_r = Rooting depth at current phenological stage (m) If θ_current < θ_FC - (RAW / (1000 × Z_r)): d_net (mm) = 1000 × (θ_FC - θ_current) × Z_r Action: Trigger Yellow Moisture Replenishment Alert.

ANNEX G PILOT PROTOCOL, EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN & ETHICS

G.1 Study population & selection criteria

The 10 pilot farms will be recruited from the Maruba/Ikiwe horticultural cluster in Machakos County under the following eligibility criteria: (1) Active irrigated cultivation of Maize or Tomato; (2) Use of pumped surface river or borehole irrigation; (3) Farm size between 0.5 and 2.5 hectares; (4) Farmer willing to participate in bi-weekly technician monitoring; (5) Signed informed consent.

G.2 Statistical analysis plan (Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test)

With 5 matched pairs, parametric normality cannot be assumed. Primary agronomic outcomes (marketable yield $t/ha$, water applied $m^3/ha$, input cost saved $USD$) will be analyzed using the Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test for Paired Non-Parametric Data ($p < 0.05$ significance threshold). Effect sizes will be reported with 95% confidence intervals rather than isolated percentage claims.
